A 3D Gaussian Splat scene is a million floating ellipsoids with color, opacity, and orientation. The naive renderer sorts all of them and draws them back-to-front every frame. That's fine for the viewer demos you see on Twitter. It is not fine when you want to bring a real-world scene into a UE5 level and stand inside it.

This is roughly the story of why XV3DGS uses a hierarchical representation.

## The naive cost model

For a scene with N splats, the per-frame cost of the naive approach is dominated by:

- Sort: roughly **O(N log N)** every frame, because the camera moves.
- Rasterization: **O(N)** Gaussians touched, plus the per-pixel blend.

When N is 200k (toy scene), the GPU happily eats it. When N is 8M (a real room captured well), every camera move drops the framerate to single digits. You can lower N — but capture quality drops linearly with it, and at that point you've lost the whole reason to use 3DGS over a textured mesh.

## What "hierarchical" buys you

Hierarchical 3DGS organizes splats into an LOD tree. Distant clusters collapse into a single coarse Gaussian; up-close clusters expand into the full detail. The renderer walks the tree once per frame and only emits the LOD level the camera actually needs.

The cost model becomes:

- Tree walk: **O(log N)** per frame.
- Rasterization: **O(visible)** rather than O(N).

For interior scenes with strong locality, "visible" is roughly an order of magnitude smaller than N. So you go from 8M-splat-rendering-at-9fps to the same scene running at 90fps in a Quest 3 headset. That's not an optimization; that's a different category of usable.

## The hard part wasn't rendering

I expected the rendering pass to be the hard part. It wasn't. The two things that ate the most time were:

1. **LOD construction at import time.** You can't hand the user a 12-hour bake. The pipeline has to construct the LOD tree in seconds-to-minutes on a workstation GPU, which means choosing your clustering algorithm carefully. K-means works; greedy spatial subdivision is faster and "good enough" for most scenes.
2. **Streaming the tree across frames.** UE5's streaming subsystem is built around meshes and textures, not splat hierarchies. The plugin has to play nicely with World Partition without pretending to be a mesh actor. Took three iterations to land on a design that doesn't fight the engine.

## Where this ends up

The unlock that matters most isn't the framerate — it's that **you can drop a real-world capture into a UE5 level and treat it like geometry**. You can light it (approximately), collide with it (via a derived proxy), and stand inside it in a headset. That's the move. Once that works, the question becomes: what do you build *next* to that capture?

In my case, the answer is a Lidar twin, an agentic cinematographer, and a commerce-grade viewer for products. But that's three more notes.
